Undergraduate research

Reading a storm
off the street

Texas A&M Hazard Reduction & Recovery Center

August 2025 – December 2025 · College Station, TX

After a hurricane, recovery money follows evidence — and evidence usually means somebody driving the streets counting debris by hand. It's slow, expensive, and two surveyors rarely agree. We taught a model to read it off street-level imagery instead.

3,142images labelled
5,200+bounding-box annotations
8disorder categories
80%mAP@0.5

Why it mattered

City planners, public health officials and emergency responders all need the same thing after a storm: an honest picture of physical disorder across a very large area, quickly. Today that assessment is a field survey — people, clipboards, weeks.

The research question was whether computer vision could stand in for that survey well enough to support real recovery decisions. Not to replace the surveyors, but to give them a first pass over a whole city in an afternoon.

The interesting constraint wasn't accuracy. It was agreement — a model is only useful here if urban planners and public health researchers accept what it calls "disorder." That definition had to be negotiated before a single box was drawn.

How it was built

1 · The data pipeline

Collected, cleaned and structured street-level imagery. Designed the annotation schema and the labelling guidelines that kept 5,200+ boxes consistent, then ran iterative quality review to squeeze out labelling noise.

2 · Model training

Fine-tuned YOLO11 on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Built reproducible training pipelines covering preprocessing, augmentation, multi-run experiments and metric-based evaluation, tracked in WandB.

3 · Working across departments

Worked with computer science, urban planning and public health. That collaboration decided which disorder categories actually mattered and kept the model's outputs pointed at real recovery use cases.

4 · Evaluation

Held-out evaluation per category rather than one headline number — a model that finds litter well and abandoned buildings badly is a very different tool than the average suggests.

What came out of it

  • Reached 80% mAP on A100 training runs, establishing a reproducible baseline the lab can iterate against.
  • Labelled ~3,000 images with 5,200+ annotations across 8 disorder categories at consistent quality.
  • Built the end-to-end training pipeline — raw data through evaluation — so future runs are repeatable rather than bespoke.
  • Contributed the ML side of an interdisciplinary team spanning CS, urban planning and public health.

Tools

The hard part was never the mAP. It was making a bounding box mean something to somebody who doesn't think in bounding boxes.
